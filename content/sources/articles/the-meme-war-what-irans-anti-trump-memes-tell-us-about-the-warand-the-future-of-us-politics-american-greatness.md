As war turns into spectacle, Trump’s meme-driven politics meets its match abroad—proving that in the attention economy, propaganda isn’t just messaging, it’s combat.

By [Raw Egg Nationalist](https://amgreatness.com/author/raw-egg-nationalist/)

April 25, 2026

With President Trump embroiled in a nasty argument with Pope Leo XIV—one that, unfortunately, could have bad consequences for Trump, who relied on Catholic voters to win key swing states like Pennsylvania and Wisconsin in 2024—it seemed things couldn’t get much worse. Trump had just posted a long rant on Truth Social in which he called the pontiff “WEAK” and “terrible” and basically a loser who should keep his nose out of things he doesn’t understand, like “setting Record Low Numbers in Crime, and creating the Greatest Stock Market in History.”

But then things did get worse. President Trump posted an AI-generated image of himself that looked suspiciously like the Son of God, tending to a sick man, surrounded by adoring onlookers and crowned with a golden halo. Cue outrage, cries of shame! and blasphemy! The meme was, in its resolute self-assurance and disregard of (literal) piety, vintage Trump.

Two days later, just as the spat between Washington and Rome seemed to be cooling off, the Iranian Embassy in Tashkent offered its own comment on the Trump-as-Jesus meme. It posted an AI-generated video of Trump’s original meme in which the real Jesus—or at least a Shiite Muslim version of him—descends from the clouds—and socks the president right in the chops, sending him face-first into the flaming depths of Hell.

“YOUR RECKONING HAS COME!” The Iranian version of Jesus booms as he sucker punches Trump from behind.

At the time of writing, this meme has 24 million views on X, 132,000 likes, and nearly 2,000 comments.

It was a bizarre intervention in what has been a pretty bizarre war. Over the last seven weeks, Iranian embassies around the world and other accounts linked to Tehran have produced memes of Trump in a rotating series of absurd guises—as a Minion from *Despicable Me*, a *Pirates of the Caribbean* caricature, and even a LEGO figure fleeing from Jeffrey Epstein.

A few of these memes have been, I am forced to admit, quite funny. Others, not so much. But I’m not the intended audience, and my opinions about the quality of these memes don’t really matter. For those who sympathize with or support the Iranian regime, hitting back at America, Israel, and President Trump with the weapon he loves most has been a great act of daring and defiance.

Despite losing almost their entire religious and military leadership in the opening hours of the war—a near-total decapitation—the Iranians have still managed to function somehow and even come up with an ad hoc social media strategy coordinated from Bulgaria to Zimbabwe, South Africa to Switzerland.

Trump’s signature America First message has now been turned on its head. Instead of being America’s savior from a corrupt globalist establishment, Trump, many now believe—let’s hope mistakenly—*is the globalist establishment*, the latest in a long line of presidents to betray the American people on behalf of a foreign power.

At times, the hostile exchange of memes has been about the only decipherable action taking place. Signal and noise have been almost impossible to separate. Is the Strait of Hormuz open or closed? Is there a blockade or are ships still delivering oil to China? You tell me, because I don’t know.

It’s all very postmodern: “hyperreal” is the word. Jean Baudrillard, the French philosopher who famously said the Gulf War never happened, would probably say the same thing about this war, too, if he were alive today. The images and symbols of the war have taken on an independent life of their own, detached from the things they’re supposed to represent. Shadow-play, but without the hands.

Memetics is warfare. For the very first time in a major conflict, propaganda is competing in the so-called “attention economy” with all the content you see when you scroll X or Instagram or TikTok: sports and podcast clips, commercials for food-delivery apps, AI slop videos—the whole sordid lot. Propaganda is now entertainment and must function as such to be successful. It must cater to the rapidly diminishing cognitive capacities of the younger generations, compressing and packaging information in ways that trigger the fleeting dopamine rush that keeps users tapping and flicking for hours at a time.

We can get carried away with this analysis, of course. Iran’s memes haven’t prevented the US from destroying the country’s leadership, air force, and navy, and reducing her ability to fire ballistic missiles at American bases and allies in the region to near zero. But Iran is still resisting, and the memes obviously serve some role in boosting morale among at least a significant portion of Iranians. In a small, maybe even a tiny way, each meme is a blow struck at America and Israel and, most of all, at Donald Trump himself.

If we look beyond the Iran War, which will probably end sooner rather than later, what can we say? What do Iran’s memes tell us about the future of American politics?

Trump’s opponents have tried to “out-meme” him before. Remember “Dark Brandon” and “Kamala is brat”? Of course you don’t, for good reason. Those memes—astroturfed creations of focus groups and committees—failed spectacularly because they were inauthentic, where almost every meme about Trump is an extension of the man himself, from portrayals of him as *Warhammer 40,000’s* God-Emperor of Mankind to AI-generated videos of him dropping poo on Harry Sisson from a plane. Iran’s memes, in their own way, have also been real—spontaneous, the product of a genuine situation and genuine need—which is why they’ve landed with such surprising force. Trump’s domestic opponents would do well to take note.

But really, the bigger issue, if we look ahead, beyond the midterms, to 2028, is that Trump won’t even be running for reelection. At present, Vice President JD Vance still seems to be the most likely candidate to replace him. The vice president already has his own unique repertoire of memes, consisting of a series of progressively more horrifyingly ugly, bald, fat, and mulleted versions of himself. While some of these memes were created to mock him, Vance has used his good humor and easygoing manner to turn them to his own advantage, including by dressing up as one of the more popular variations last Halloween. Even so, it’s not quite clear that Vance has the meme magic Trump has, at least not yet.

I mean, who does?

If it’s Kamala Harris again in 2028, I don’t think JD Vance has anything to worry about. Gavin Newscum, on the other hand, as Trump likes to refer to him, might be a cause for concern. He’s already done a fine turn as *American Psycho*’s Patrick Bateman, and he understands as well as any leftist politician the power of image. If he can get his meme game on point, he could be in with a serious chance of winning—unlike Iran.
