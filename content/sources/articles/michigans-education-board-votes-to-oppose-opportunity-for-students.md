Michigan school board fights help for vulnerable students

Kaitlyn Buss discusses State Board of Education's pushback against federal scholarship program

Michigan’s State Board of Education has once again chosen ideology over students.

In a resolution passed this week, the board formally opposed efforts to have Michigan participate in the new federal Educational Choice for Children Act scholarship program — a voluntary initiative that would allow private donations to fund scholarships for K-12 students through federal tax credits.

The reaction from the board was revealing.

Rather than approaching the proposal with curiosity or even cautious openness, members immediately framed it as a threat to public education. It has been mischaracterized a voucher program and members are concerned it would drain resources from public schools.

But why shouldn’t Michigan at least try every available option to help children succeed? Especially now.

![Kindergarten students sit on a classroom floor at McKinley.](/d7mqH/94a0c18015398c297f42973d848813f0e465865a.webp)

Michigan’s academic performance remains among the nation’s worst. Reading and math scores continue to lag. Chronic absenteeism exploded after the pandemic. Parents increasingly express frustration with school environments, curriculum disputes and a system that too often appears more focused on institutional preservation than student outcomes.

And yet when presented with a program that could potentially expand opportunities for lower- and middle-income families — funded not by Lansing tax hikes but through incentivized private donations — the board’s instinct was not exploration. It was opposition.

That says a great deal.

More: [Whitmer is right to wait on federal school choice plan | Opinion](https://archive.is/o/d7mqH/https://www.detroitnews.com/story/opinion/2026/04/29/gov-gretchen-whitmer-is-right-to-wait-on-federal-school-choice-plan-decision/89837440007/)

The Educational Choice for Children Act would allow states to opt into a federal tax-credit scholarship system where donors receive credits for contributing to scholarship-granting organizations. Those organizations would then help eligible students afford tuition, tutoring, special-needs services, homeschooling expenses and other educational options.

Importantly, states are not forced to participate. Michigan can choose whether to opt in, which makes the board’s resolution even more absurd.

What is the danger in allowing Michigan families access to additional educational opportunities? Why should bureaucrats in Lansing decide that struggling students must remain trapped in one system, regardless of whether it is meeting their needs?

The board insists its opposition stems from protecting public schools. But public education exists to serve students — not the other way around. That distinction matters.

Too often in Michigan, education debates have become centered on protecting institutions, employees and political interests rather than asking what arrangements best help individual children flourish.

And the reality is that many families already exercise school choice every day — through private schools, charter schools, homeschooling or by moving to districts they perceive as better. Wealthier families have long possessed educational options. Programs like these merely attempt to extend some measure of flexibility to families without those advantages.

More: [Michigan State Board of Education shouldn't block school choice | Opinion](https://archive.is/o/d7mqH/https://www.detroitnews.com/story/opinion/2026/04/20/bussard-michigan-state-board-of-education-shouldnt-block-school-choice-opinion/89646089007/)

The board’s resolution also reflects a broader ideological rigidity that increasingly dominates education politics in Michigan.

Any proposal involving parental choice is immediately labeled an attack on public education. There is little acknowledgment that many parents simply want alternatives because they feel the current system is failing their children academically, socially or morally.

Nor is there much humility from a public education establishment presiding over years of declining outcomes.

Michigan should absolutely scrutinize the details of any federal program. Questions about accountability, implementation and oversight are legitimate. But reflexively opposing even the possibility of expanded educational opportunity before the state has fully debated it demonstrates a defensive posture that has become all too common among education officials.

At some point, leaders must decide whether their priority is protecting systems or empowering families.

The State Board of Education made its choice this week. Lawmakers and Gov. Gretchen Whitmer should make a different one.
