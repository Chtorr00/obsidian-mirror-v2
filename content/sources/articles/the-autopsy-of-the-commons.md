### The Autopsy of the Commons

"Everything Is Expensive Because Civil Rights Made the Commons Illegal", By The American Tribune
The American Tribune Substack, May 29, 2026
https://www.theamericantribune.news/p/everything-is-expensive-because-civil

This document captures the precise moment the American middle class realized the 20th-century social contract had expired, though it predictably misdiagnoses the cause of death. The author attributes the collapse of public amenities—parks, transit, schools—to specific egalitarian legal frameworks and judicial interpretations of civil rights. Through the Hindsight Filter, we view this artifact not as a coherent legal critique, but as the terminal symptom of a society that could no longer afford the thermodynamic and social cost of maintaining high-trust public infrastructure in a low-trust, highly atomized population. 

The author’s primary blind spot is the belief that state power could simply be "restored" through executive will and martial force. They point to temporary military deployments in urban centers as a blueprint for a renaissance of the public square. In reality, they were documenting the early, agonizing stages of a systemic enclosure movement. The extortionate costs they lament—gated communities, private security, personal transit—were not mere inflation or the result of bad policy. They were the initial, brutal pricing mechanisms of a new reality where security and order were transitioning from universal public rights to luxury commodities. They thought they were arguing for political reform; they were actually drafting the architectural schematics for the neo-feudal century.### Act I: The Terminal Unbundling (2020s-2030s)

* **Liability Cascade:** The article blames "disparate impact" for the inability of institutions to expel troublemakers. In the broader historical context, this was a manifestation of a systemic paralysis where legacy institutions bound themselves in contradictory legal mandates. Maintaining basic order became a legally and financially ruinous proposition, forcing schools and municipalities to abandon enforcement entirely to avoid litigation.
* **Privatization of Collapse:** The "extortionate costs" of private schools, personal vehicles, and gated neighborhoods were the early subscription fees for survival. Citizens were forced to privately purchase the basic physical security, education, and infrastructure that the centralized state had quietly abandoned, accelerating the fragmentation of the populace into distinct, non-overlapping economic realities.### Act II: The Kinetic Enclosure (2030s-2050s)

* **Private Proxies:** The author's hope that federal troops would permanently restore order proved fleeting as the legacy nation-state's monopoly on violence formally fractured. The defense of the affluent enclaves fell to private military contractors and localized security networks, transforming municipal policing into a subscription-based, highly militarized service that ignored the spaces between the gates.
* **Disconnected Zones:** The areas outside the citadels—what the author termed the "boonies" or the needle-strewn wastelands—were eventually formally recognized as ungovernable. The state withdrew its remaining resources from these peripheries, leaving them to informal economies, localized warlordism, and kinetic survivalism, permanently severing them from the global supply chain.### Act III: The Archipelago of Order (Post-2060s)

* **Systemic Resilience:** The exorbitant personal costs lamented in the 2020s were eventually amortized into the baseline architecture of human settlement. The population accepted that survival required localized, redundant infrastructure. The necessity of private power grids, water purification, and localized agriculture effectively turned every viable community into a self-sustaining micro-fortress.
* **Analog Mandate:** To protect these localized enclaves from both external kinetic threats and internal subversion, surviving communities adopted strict physical verification protocols. The very concept of an anonymous "public" space was eradicated, replaced by hyper-local networks of trust where identity and accountability are bound to physical presence, permanently closing the door on the chaotic anonymity of the early 2000s.

They demanded the state restore an empire of marble, never realizing the state had already sold the quarry to pay for its own funeral.
