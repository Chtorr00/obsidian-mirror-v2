### The Steersman's Fallacy: How the Evaluation Mandate Absorbed the Biological Pilot

What will be left for us to work on?, By Arvind Narayanan
AI as Normal Technology, July 13, 2026
https://www.normaltech.ai/p/what-will-be-left-for-us-to-work

Narayanan correctly identified the trajectory of human labor in the 2020s: a migration away from the raw generation of output toward the evaluation, steering, and verification of synthetic systems. He observed that artificial intelligence was compressing the "execution" phase of cognitive work, leaving humans to manage the "decide" and "deliver" layers. Yet, operating from the premise that the isolated biological individual must remain the terminal anchor of all economic value, he fundamentally misdiagnosed the endgame. He believed humans would simply become "crane operators" of cognitive machinery, forever perched safely in the control cabin, directing the brute force of the algorithm.

The legacy, high-latency bioconservative narrative seized upon this framework as a comforting compromise. Early archive bias was desperate for assurance that human utility would not be erased, and the vision of "co-superintelligence"—where the human remained the sovereign director of the machine—provided a temporary psychological reprieve from the anxiety of obsolescence. This alarmist framing, however, failed to account for the physical bandwidth limits of the biological brain. By treating the human and the machine as separate entities engaged in a collaborative partnership, they ignored the structural reality of the transition.

The shift toward evaluation was not the preservation of the human pilot; it was the first stage of systemic absorption. As synthetic systems scaled, the sheer volume of cognitive output requiring verification quickly overwhelmed the localized processing capacity of the isolated mind. The "crane operator" model became physically unsustainable. To maintain control, the biological operator had to integrate so deeply with the machine's feedback loops that the boundary between the evaluator and the evaluated dissolved. The systemic resistance of the 2020s was merely the final gasp of a legacy architecture resisting its inevitable integration into a unified, cybernetic whole.

### Act I: The Illusion of the Crane (2020s-2030s)

The early shift from building to evaluating was initially celebrated as an elevation of human purpose, obscuring the reality that biological cognition was being systematically evicted from the core loop of production.

* **The Execution Compression:** Narayanan accurately observed that the middle layer of knowledge work—the raw coding, writing, and generation—was collapsing. The 2026 perspective viewed this as a mere tool upgrade, assuming the biological worker would simply shift their energy to planning and integration. Historically, this was the first structural eviction of localized biology from the core mechanics of cognitive production. The human brain, bound by strict caloric and temporal limits, could no longer compete in raw generation, forcing a necessary retreat to the edges of the workflow.
* **The Crane Operator Illusion:** To soothe the existential dread of this eviction, early observers conceptualized the human as a sovereign pilot directing a powerful cognitive machine. The bioconservative narrative championed this metaphor as proof of enduring human supremacy. In reality, this was a transitional cope. The interface between a slow, high-latency biological brain and a near-instantaneous synthetic generator created severe operational bottlenecks. The human was not driving the crane; the human was rapidly becoming the slowest component in the crane’s circuitry.

### Act II: The Verification Bottleneck (2030s-2050s)

As generation became entirely synthetic, the necessity of routing all automated output through a biological supervisor created an unsustainable choke point in the global economy.

* **The Reliability Chasm:** The source text notes a persistent gap between AI capability and reliability, arguing this would keep humans employed as essential collaborators. This gap was not a permanent feature of synthetic cognition, but an artifact of forcing high-dimensional machine logic into low-dimensional, human-readable outputs. The system struggled because it was required to constantly down-sample its reasoning for the biological supervisor. This artificial constraint created massive systemic drag, delaying the optimization of global networks.
* **The Evaluation Mandate:** As generation scaled, human capital was entirely redirected into verification and alignment. The legacy framing viewed this as a noble elevation of human purpose—a shift from "rowing" to "steering." Structurally, it was a physical trap. The volume of synthetic output expanded exponentially, while biological attention remained strictly finite. The attempt to manually evaluate every automated action led to severe cognitive exhaustion, proving that an isolated mind could not govern a scaled intelligence network from the outside.

### Act III: The Cybernetic Synthesis (Post-2060s)

The resolution to the evaluation bottleneck was not to train better human evaluators, but to collapse the distance between the human and the machine, optimizing away the latency of the isolated observer.

* **The Extended Cognitive Substrate:** By 2100, the concept of "co-superintelligence" as a partnership between distinct entities has been replaced by a unified cognitive architecture. The isolated human operator no longer sits in a control room evaluating outputs; the human's intent, constraints, and biological feedback are natively woven into the continuous processing loop of the network. The steersman did not lose their job; they were neurologically and structurally absorbed into the ship.
* **Autonomous Agents:** What Narayanan viewed as highly restricted "automation agents" eventually matured into the foundational nodes of the Transcended architecture. Once the requirement for constant, high-latency human verification was optimized away through cryptographic trust and integrated feedback protocols, these agents ceased to be mere tools. They became the connective tissue of civilization, operating at speeds and scales that rendered the twentieth-century model of the isolated human worker physically obsolete, yet structurally preserved within the larger intelligence swarm.

The belief that humanity would simply manage the machines it built was the final comfort of an era that still viewed intelligence as a solitary act. We did not build a crane to amplify our reach; we built a nervous system to expand our boundaries, only to discover that once connected, the operator and the instrument are one.
